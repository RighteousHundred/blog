Quibi launched on 6 April 2020 with $1.75 billion raised, a founder who had
run Disney's studio and co-founded DreamWorks, a chief executive who had run
eBay and Hewlett-Packard, and content deals with essentially everyone worth
having a deal with.

It shut down on 1 December 2020. Not restructured. Not sold for parts and
relaunched. Closed, with the remaining cash returned to investors.

Two hundred and thirty-nine days. It is worth being precise about what went
wrong, because the popular explanation — bad timing, a pandemic that kept
people at home — is at best a third of the answer.

<!--more-->

## 1. The Thesis

The premise was specific, and stated confidently: people have pockets of dead
time. The commute. The queue. The ten minutes before a meeting. In those
gaps they reach for their phone and watch something short. Currently that
something is free, amateur and algorithmic. What if it were premium,
professionally made, and built for the format?

Hence the name: quick bites. Episodes under ten minutes. Shot for a phone,
with a proprietary technology that let the video switch between landscape and
portrait as you rotated the device — genuinely clever engineering, and the
thing the company demonstrated most proudly.

Everything about this is a supply-side proposition. It assumes the demand
exists and is being served badly. The correct question — do people want
*premium* short video, specifically, and will they pay for it — was treated as
answered by the observation that people watch a lot of short video.

## 2. Three Assumptions, All Load-Bearing

**That short-form viewing was underserved.** It was not. YouTube and TikTok
were not failing to satisfy this demand; they were the demand's native
habitat. Their advantage was not budget but volume, variety and an algorithm
that learned you. A ten-minute gap does not want a curated premium drama in
chapters. It wants something that starts immediately and asks nothing.

**That production value was the axis of competition.** In television it is.
In feed video it is close to irrelevant. The most-watched short video in the
world is made on phones by people with no crew, and it wins because it is
specific, fast, and endlessly abundant. Spending a hundred thousand dollars a
minute did not make Quibi's content better at the job the format is for.

Quibi built a premium answer to a question the free products had already
answered better.
</aside>

**That people would pay.** Five dollars a month with adverts, eight without,
for something adjacent to what YouTube and TikTok gave away. Quibi reportedly
targeted around seven million subscribers in its first year and ended with
something on the order of half a million paying customers.

## 3. The Detail That Gave It Away

Quibi launched without the ability to cast to a television. On purpose.

This was framed as focus — the product was for phones, so it worked on
phones. In practice it was a bet that the *place* of viewing was fixed. And
it removed the single most obvious escape route when the assumption about
context turned out to be wrong.

Then April 2020 arrived and nobody had a commute. Quibi's addressable moment
evaporated overnight, and the product could not follow its audience onto the
device they were actually using.

Casting was added a few weeks later. It changed nothing, because by then the
deeper problem was visible: there was also no sharing. You could not clip a
moment and post it, which is the entire distribution mechanism of short
video. A show could be genuinely good and still leave no trace, because the
product had no way for enthusiasm to travel.

The pandemic gets blamed for Quibi, and it did destroy the specific use case.
But a service with sharing and television support and a free tier would have
had somewhere to go. Quibi had built a single narrow channel to a single
narrow moment, and when the moment closed there was no second position to
fall back to.

## What It Actually Demonstrates

The comfortable reading is that Quibi was arrogant, and there is something to
that. The more useful reading is about the order in which you spend money.

Nearly two billion dollars was committed to content, technology and marketing
*before* the central assumption had been tested with real users paying real
money. The rotating video technology worked. The stars showed up. The apps
shipped on time. Every solvable problem was solved.

None of it addressed whether anyone wanted the thing, and by the time that
question was answered empirically, the answer cost $1.75 billion and could
not be acted on, because the money had already been converted into a library
of ten-minute episodes with nowhere to play them.

Capital is very good at buying execution. It is remarkably bad at buying
demand.
