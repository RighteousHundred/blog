Between 2020 and 2022, roughly ten billion dollars went into companies
promising groceries at your door in fifteen minutes or less. Gorillas.
Getir. Jokr. Buyk. Fridge No More. Zapp. Dija. Weezy. Flink.

By 2024, most of them were gone, merged into each other, or had retreated to
a single home market. The interesting part is that the operational promise
was kept. They really did deliver in ten to fifteen minutes. The delivery
worked. The arithmetic underneath it did not.

<!--more-->

## 1. The Model on Paper

The idea was a genuine improvement on what came before.

Traditional grocery delivery picks orders from a supermarket built for
humans pushing trolleys — wide aisles, product placed for browsing, staff
walking miles per shift. It is slow and expensive. So: build a *dark store*
instead. A small warehouse, maybe two to four thousand square feet, no
customers, laid out purely for picking speed. Stock two or three thousand of
the fastest-moving items rather than thirty thousand. Put it in the middle of
a dense residential district. A rider can reach any address within a mile in
a few minutes.

Every piece of that is sound. Picking times genuinely fell to a couple of
minutes. Delivery radii genuinely were short. The companies were not lying
about the operations.

The trouble is what sits on either side of them.

## 2. The Cost Floor

Work out what it costs to deliver one basket.

A rider has to be paid, and paid whether or not an order is waiting. In most
European markets these companies employed riders rather than using
contractors, which was better for the riders and much worse for the
flexibility of the cost base. Call the fully loaded cost of a rider somewhere
around fifteen to twenty euros an hour once you include wages, insurance,
equipment and the vehicle.

Now: how many deliveries can that rider complete in an hour? The whole
proposition is single-order, direct-to-door, ten minutes. Round trip plus
handover plus waiting means three, maybe four an hour in good conditions.

So the rider cost alone lands somewhere in the region of four to six euros
per order, before the dark store's rent, before the pickers inside it, before
the goods themselves, before customer acquisition.

<aside class="pullquote" markdown="1">
The average basket was around twenty euros. The gross margin on groceries is
in the mid-twenties. There was never enough there.
</aside>

Against that, the average basket in this category ran around fifteen to
twenty-five euros — people were ordering the things you order when you want
them *now*, which is a small number of items. Grocery gross margins sit in
the mid-twenties as a percentage. Twenty euros of groceries yields perhaps
five euros of gross profit.

Five euros of gross profit against four to six euros of rider cost, and the
store, the pickers, and the marketing all still unpaid. The gap was not a
matter of tuning. It was the whole business.

## 3. Density Was the Answer, and It Wasn't Enough

Every operator knew this, and every operator gave the same answer: density.

The logic is real. If enough orders arrive from the same neighbourhood at the
same time, a rider can carry two or three at once and the cost per drop falls
sharply. Fixed costs at the dark store spread over more baskets. Utilisation
of riders rises so they wait less.

This is exactly how the profitable end of delivery works. A parcel courier
does not carry one box per trip; it carries a van full, and the economics
come almost entirely from drops per hour.

But batching orders and delivering in ten minutes are directly opposed. The
moment you hold an order to pair it with another, you have spent the promise
you sold. The speed that differentiated the product was the same thing
preventing the cost structure from working.

So operators were left pushing on the other variable: raising basket size,
through minimum orders, delivery fees, and range expansion. Each of those
made the product more like ordinary grocery delivery, which is a competitive,
low-margin business already occupied by supermarkets with existing supply
chains and no need to fund a separate network of dark stores.

## What Survived

The consolidation was rapid. Getir bought Gorillas at the end of 2022. Jokr
exited the United States. Buyk shut down. Getir itself withdrew from the US,
UK and remaining European markets in 2024, retreating to Turkey, where it had
density, scale and a market structure that suited it.

What persists is the pieces that were always sound. Dark stores are now
standard infrastructure for large grocers doing online fulfilment. Batching
and route optimisation, developed under this pressure, run in every delivery
app. Fifteen-minute delivery survives as a premium tier with a fee attached,
which is to say it survives as what it always was: a service some people will
pay the real cost for, occasionally.

What did not survive was the belief that a structurally expensive way of
moving goods would become cheap if enough capital were pointed at it. The last
mile has a floor. You can lower it with density, batching and scale, and the
best operators in the world have spent decades doing exactly that. What you
cannot do is push it below the gross margin on a bag of groceries.
