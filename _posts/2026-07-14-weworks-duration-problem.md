The story most people remember about WeWork is the personality: the founder,
the tequila, the private jet, the S-1 that used the word "elevate" a
suspicious number of times, the $47 billion valuation that evaporated in
about six weeks in 2019.

That story is entertaining and largely beside the point. Strip out every
eccentricity and you are left with a company that did one thing: it signed
very long leases on office space and sold very short ones. Everything that
happened afterwards follows from that sentence.

<!--more-->

## 1. The Spread

The pitch was clean. Landlords want long, boring, creditworthy tenants and
will discount heavily to get them. Small companies and freelancers want a
desk for a few months with no commitment, and will pay a premium for the
privilege. Sit between the two, take the difference, add coffee and a
community manager.

This is real. The spread genuinely exists. Serviced-office companies have
made money from it for decades — IWG, the company behind Regus, had been
doing a version of it since the late 1980s.

WeWork's innovation was not the spread. It was the speed. Rather than
growing into the arbitrage, it took roughly $12 billion of investor capital,
most conspicuously from SoftBank, and signed as many leases as it could as
fast as it could, on the theory that scale would eventually produce pricing
power, brand, and better terms from landlords.

By 2019 it had become one of the largest private office tenants in London,
Manhattan and several other cities, with lease obligations running to well
over ten billion dollars stretching out over a decade and a half.

Against which it sold memberships that a customer could cancel with a
month's notice.

## 2. Why This Is a Bank

Line those two things up and you have described a maturity transformation:
long-dated liabilities funded by short-dated commitments.

Banks do exactly this. They borrow short — deposits you can withdraw today —
and lend long, in thirty-year mortgages. The spread between the two is most
of retail banking. It is also why banks fail: if enough depositors ask for
their money at once, the assets cannot be liquidated fast enough to meet the
claims, and an institution that was solvent on paper on Monday is gone by
Friday.

Because this failure mode is so well understood, banks are wrapped in
protections. Capital requirements. Liquidity coverage ratios. Deposit
insurance to stop the run before it starts. A central bank that will lend
against good collateral when nobody else will. Every one of those exists
because someone learned the hard way what maturity mismatch does under
stress.

WeWork ran a bank's balance sheet with a hospitality company's regulation.
</aside>

WeWork ran the same structure with none of it. Its liabilities were
contractual and fixed. Its revenues were cancellable and discretionary. And
its customers were disproportionately startups and small firms — the segment
whose demand falls first and hardest in a downturn.

The company's own disclosures leaned on this being manageable because leases
were signed through individual subsidiaries, limiting recourse. That is a
real mitigation for the parent's balance sheet. It does nothing for the
underlying economics, which is that you have promised to pay rent for fifteen
years on space whose occupants can leave in thirty days.

## 3. The Stress Test Nobody Ordered

Then 2020 happened, and the entire developed world ran the experiment.

Offices emptied. The short side of the book — the members — did what short
commitments allow you to do. The long side did not move at all, because
fifteen-year leases are not sensitive to whether anyone is using the space.

WeWork spent the following years renegotiating. It walked away from
locations, restructured hundreds of leases, and got real concessions from
landlords who preferred a discount to an empty floor. Under new management it
made genuine progress on occupancy and cost. None of it was enough, because
none of it could change the shape of the mismatch — it could only shrink it.

The company went public through a SPAC in October 2021 at a valuation a
fraction of its private peak, and filed for Chapter 11 in November 2023,
explicitly citing its lease obligations.

## What Was Actually Wrong

It is tempting to file this under hubris, and there was plenty. But IWG,
running a similar model with more conservative lease terms, a franchise-heavy
structure and far less capital, came through the same period intact.

The difference was leverage against a known fragility. WeWork did not
discover a flawed business model. It took a functioning one, removed the
caution that made it work, and scaled the result with cheap capital until the
mismatch was large enough to be fatal.

The spread between long leases and short memberships is real money. It is
also the compensation you are paid for bearing a specific risk. WeWork spent
a decade collecting that compensation while insisting the risk was somebody
else's problem, and then, in the spring of 2020, found out whose it was.
