Bird went from founding to a billion-dollar valuation faster than any company
in American history. It took about fourteen months. By 2018 there were
scooters on the pavements of a hundred cities and a genuine sense that urban
transport had just changed.

The business rested on an equation with four terms: what a scooter costs,
what it earns per day, how much it costs to run each day, and how many days
it lasts. Three of those were knowable from the start. The fourth was not,
and it was the one that mattered.

<!--more-->

## 1. The Equation

Take the early fleets, the consumer-grade scooters bought off the shelf and
fitted with a lock and a GPS unit. Call it four hundred dollars a unit,
roughly, in 2018.

Revenue per scooter per day was the number everyone quoted, and in a good
city in good weather it ran around four to five dollars — a dollar to unlock
plus fifteen cents a minute, a handful of rides.

Against that sat daily operating cost. Every scooter has to be collected,
charged and redistributed overnight. Early on this was outsourced to gig
workers paid five dollars or so per scooter charged. Add repairs,
redistribution vans, city permit fees and insurance.

You can see immediately that the daily margin is thin — perhaps a dollar or
two in a good market. Which means the entire return depends on the last term.
At two dollars a day of contribution, a four-hundred-dollar scooter needs two
hundred days of service just to return its purchase price.

## 2. The Number Nobody Had

So how long does a shared scooter last?

Nobody knew, because nobody had ever left thousands of consumer scooters
outdoors, unattended, in public, ridden by strangers with no stake in their
survival.

The early answers were grim. Independent analyses in 2018 and 2019, working
from city permit data and vehicle identification numbers, suggested average
lifespans measured in the low months — some estimates as short as one to three
months in the harshest markets. Scooters were ridden into kerbs, left in the
rain, thrown into canals, stripped for parts, and simply worn out by a duty
cycle they were never designed for. A consumer scooter is built for one owner
doing a couple of miles a day and storing it indoors. These were doing
dozens of rides a day and sleeping outside.

<aside class="pullquote" markdown="1">
A four-hundred-dollar asset earning two dollars a day needs two hundred days.
The early fleets were not getting two hundred days.
</aside>

Run the arithmetic with a ninety-day life and every scooter is a loss before
a single overhead cost is counted. Growth makes it worse, exactly as it did
for the indoor farms and the delivery apps: each new city meant more capital
converted into assets that would be scrap before they broke even.

This is the specific shape of the failure. It was not that riders would not
pay, or that demand was absent. Demand was extraordinary. It was that the
company was, in effect, renting out a depreciating asset for less than its
depreciation.

## 3. The Fix, and Who Could Afford It

The industry's answer was correct and expensive: build a better scooter.

By 2019 and 2020 the operators were designing their own hardware — heavier
frames, swappable batteries, industrial components, self-diagnostics. Claimed
lifespans went from months to years. Swappable batteries removed the entire
nightly collection cycle, which had been one of the largest operating costs;
a van could swap batteries in the street instead of hauling scooters to a
warehouse.

Those changes worked. They also required capital, engineering, and several
years of patience, all of which had to be spent while the existing fleet was
still losing money.

That is the point at which the market sorted itself. Lime, having raised
heavily and moved early to purpose-built hardware and swappable batteries,
reported positive operating results and survived. Bird, which had grown
fastest and burned the most on the disposable fleet, restructured
repeatedly, was delisted, and filed for Chapter 11 in December 2023. A long
tail of smaller operators disappeared in city licensing rounds that
increasingly favoured whoever could demonstrate durable equipment.

## The Underlying Rule

Any business that rents a physical asset lives or dies on the ratio between
what the asset earns over its life and what it cost to buy. Aircraft
leasing, car rental, construction plant, laundromats — they are all the same
calculation, and the mature versions of those industries are obsessive about
asset life because it is the term with the most leverage.

The scooter companies launched before that number existed. They estimated it
from indoor use of consumer hardware and were badly wrong, in the direction
that costs the most money, at a moment when capital was cheap enough to hide
the error for two full years.

The survivors are the ones that treated the lifespan as the product — because
in a rental business, it is.
