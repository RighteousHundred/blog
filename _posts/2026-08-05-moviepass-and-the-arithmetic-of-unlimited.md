In August 2017, a struggling subscription service cut its price to $9.95 a
month and offered, in exchange, a cinema ticket every single day. The average
American cinema ticket that year cost about nine dollars.

You do not need a spreadsheet to see the shape of this. One visit and the
month is a wash. Two visits and the company is underwater. What is
interesting is not that MoviePass failed — it is *how fast* the mechanism
worked, and why growth made it worse rather than better.

<!--more-->

## 1. The Offer That Sold Itself

Before the price cut, MoviePass had spent five years failing to matter. It had
something in the region of twenty thousand subscribers. Within a day of the
announcement its servers buckled. Within a year it claimed more than three
million.

That is the fastest consumer subscription ramp anyone had seen outside of
free products, and it happened for an obvious reason: the offer was worth
more than it cost. Not "felt like good value" — *was worth more*, arithmetically,
to anyone who went to the cinema more than once a month.

The company's stated theory was that this did not matter, because MoviePass
was not really in the ticket business. It was buying an audience. With
millions of subscribers it would have leverage over exhibitors, who would
share concession revenue or sell tickets at a discount. It would have data
about what people watched, which studios would pay for. The tickets were
customer acquisition cost, and acquisition cost is supposed to be temporary.

Every part of that theory required MoviePass to eventually pay less than face
value for a ticket. It never did, at anything like the scale required. Cinema
chains had no reason to help. AMC, the largest, publicly objected to the
$9.95 price almost immediately and later launched its own subscription — one
with a crucial difference we will come back to.

## 2. Adverse Selection Is Not a Rounding Error

Here is where the model stops being merely unprofitable and becomes
structurally impossible.

An insurance company can sell a policy that loses money on some customers,
because the customers who file claims are outnumbered by the customers who
do not. The whole business is built on that ratio, and insurers spend
enormous effort pricing it correctly.

MoviePass was, functionally, selling insurance against the price of cinema
tickets. But it had inverted the ratio deliberately. Who signs up for
unlimited cinema? People who go to the cinema. The heavier the moviegoer, the
more obviously attractive the offer, and the faster they subscribe.

This is adverse selection, and at MoviePass it was not a tail risk — it was
the entire acquisition funnel. Every marketing dollar spent bringing in more
subscribers was, on average, buying customers who would use the product more
than the previous cohort, because the light users had less reason to bother.

So the per-subscriber loss did not stabilise as the company scaled. There was
no volume at which the numbers turned. A normal business with negative gross
margin at least holds its loss per unit constant while it works on cost;
MoviePass's loss per unit had a tendency to *grow* with the size of the base.

The company's responses tell you it understood this late. It throttled. It
introduced surge pricing, blackouts on major releases, verification photos of
ticket stubs, limits dressed as features. In July 2018 it briefly ran out of
money mid-weekend and had to take an emergency loan to keep processing
tickets during the opening of a major film. Then it capped subscribers at
three films a month, which is to say it stopped selling the thing people had
bought.

## 3. What AMC Understood

The instructive comparison is AMC Stubs A-List, launched in June 2018 at
roughly $20 a month for three films a week.

It looks like the same product. It is a completely different business.

AMC owns the cinemas. When an A-List subscriber attends, AMC does not pay
anyone for a ticket — it forgoes revenue on a seat that, on a Tuesday
afternoon, was overwhelmingly likely to be empty anyway. The marginal cost of
seating one more person in a room that is already lit, staffed and screening
is close to nothing. And that person buys popcorn, where the margins are the
reason cinemas exist at all.

MoviePass had none of this. It sat outside the value chain paying retail
into it, hoping that volume would eventually buy it a seat at the table. AMC
sat inside the value chain, converting empty inventory into a recurring
payment and a concession customer.

The difference is not strategy or execution. It is that one company was
discounting its own spare capacity and the other was buying someone else's
at full price.

## What It Cost

Helios & Matheson Analytics, MoviePass's majority owner, burned through cash
at a rate that became a running joke in financial press — tens of millions a
month at the peak. The stock, after reverse splits, went to essentially
nothing. The service shut down in September 2019, and the parent company
filed for Chapter 7 in January 2020.

The lesson is narrower and more useful than "don't sell below cost".
Plenty of businesses sell below cost on purpose and survive; that is what a
loss-leader is. The condition is that the loss has to be bounded, and the
customers it attracts have to be worth more later than they cost now.

MoviePass violated both. The loss scaled with usage, usage scaled with
enthusiasm, and enthusiasm was exactly what the marketing was buying.
